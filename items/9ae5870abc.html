2026 年 11 月 2 日，一家自述服务超 20 万客户的大型托管服务商遭到针对其管理服务器的攻击，脚本擦除了数据。到 11 月 6 日，该服务商称其云基础设施已被摧毁、正在重建，文件备份仅约一半虚拟服务器用户还有。

公开已知的情况（截至 2026 年 11 月 8 日）：时间线方面，服务商的记录称攻击约在 11 月 2 日 02:00（莫斯科时间）开始，约 40 分钟后被发现，首次客户通知在 04:50。备份方面，11 月 3 日服务商称文件备份幸存，但虚拟服务器的二进制备份丢失，损失波及虚拟服务器、对象存储与 Kubernetes，CDN 设置与 IP 地址也丢失；11 月 6 日进一步说明，约一半虚拟服务器用户有文件备份，部分关联账号与服务的记录缺失。恢复方面，11 月 6 日共享主机恢复比例据报超过 60%，11 月 7 日服务商称控制面板部分恢复，剩余共享主机站点的备份恢复已经开始，尚无完成日期。原因方面，无组织认领，无赎金要求报道，入侵途径未披露；服务商称未发现客户数据被下载或篡改的证据——这是服务商的结论，不是证明。下游影响包括一个国家产品标签系统的公共门户（核心系统继续运行）、一家汽车共享服务、一项支付服务与数个地区政府网站，其中汽车共享服务当天下午通过转到另一家承包商恢复了服务。合同方面，服务商宣布不可抗力并暂停了尚未恢复服务的计费；评论指出，合同赔偿一般按已付费用计算，而非按业务损失。

七条教训：

1. 留一份生产管理员删不掉的恢复副本。已报道的模式是攻击者进入管理层并用它擦除数据。如果运行生产的凭据也能删除备份、目录与密钥，备份就处在波及范围内。直接问一个问题：被攻陷的生产管理员能否删除组织的恢复副本？用单独的凭据保留一份隔离或不可变的副本，并按计划从中恢复。

2. 保护地图，而不只是数据。服务商报告了关联账号与服务的记录缺失。没有“文件属于谁、属于哪个服务”的记录，文件更难归还、更难证明。目录、配置、资产清单与解密密钥需要自己的副本与保护。

3. 服务商的百分比不是组织的恢复。“恢复超 60%”统计的是共享主机服务器，不是组织的业务服务。一个站点恢复了，其数据库、邮件历史、DNS 或集成可能还没恢复。按每个工作负载、对照组织设定的时间衡量恢复。ISO 22301:2019 要求组织分析中断对每项活动的影响，包括对供应商的依赖（8.2.2），并选择连续性策略（8.3）。最有用的数字是：这家服务商可能造成的最长中断，对照组织某项活动能容忍的最长中断。

4. 事先知道退出路径。事故中汽车共享服务几小时内就转到了另一家承包商，这只有在第二条路线事先准备好的情况下才可行：谁控制域名、DNS 如何变更、内容副本在哪里、谁能批准迁移。ISO/IEC 27001:2022 附录 A 要求对供应商关系与云服务设控制（A.5.19 至 A.5.23）；ISO/IEC 20000-1:2018 要求组织管理供应商（8.3.4）。

5. 在断服之前，按损失读合同。如果一天停机造成的损失超过一年的费用，按费用计算的补救与连续性计划并不匹配。还要检查组织给自己客户的承诺是否超过供应商给组织的承诺，包括不可抗力排除条款。这是通用做法，不是法律建议。

6. 演练完整重建，而不只是恢复文件。仅有文件成不了服务。一次重建覆盖服务器、配置、DNS、证书、密钥与数据，最后由业务用户确认可用，并计时一次。

7. 规划组织的对外沟通并保留损失记录。服务商的首次客户通知在估计的开始时间后不到三小时发出。组织的客户应当更早听到消息，渠道不能依赖同一家服务商（如果邮件跑在同一家服务商上，它就不是一条独立渠道）。保留损失内容与时间的记录，用于索赔与保险。

作者还给了一个下周可跑的桌面演练：虚构服务商、四次注入——T+0 站点与邮件中断且状态页打不开；T+3 小时服务商确认管理层被未授权访问、数据被擦除且无恢复时间；T+24 小时服务商称备份只覆盖部分客户且组织未被确认在内；T+72 小时第二环境已用组织的副本运行，客户要求证明没有内容被篡改并问何时完全恢复。把恢复时间目标与实际重建时间的差距记为发现项，每项指定负责人与日期。

原文

On 2 November 2026 a large hosting provider, which says it serves more than 200,000 customers, was hit by an attack on its management servers. Scripts erased data. By 6 November the provider said its cloud infrastructure had been destroyed and was being rebuilt, and that file backups would exist for only about half of its virtual server users.

What is known, as of 8 November 2026: Timeline. The provider's chronology puts the start at about 02:00 on 2 November (Moscow time), detection about 40 minutes later and the first customer notice at 04:50. Backups. On 3 November it said file backups survived but binary backups of virtual servers were lost, with losses across virtual servers, object storage and Kubernetes. CDN settings and IP addresses were also lost. On 6 November it narrowed the position: file backups for about half of virtual server users, and some records linking accounts to services missing. Recovery. Shared hosting was reported above 60% restored on 6 November. On 7 November the provider said its control panel was partly working and that restoring the remaining shared-hosting sites from backups had started, with no completion date. Cause. No group has claimed responsibility, no ransom demand has been reported and the way in is undisclosed. The provider says it found no evidence that customer data was downloaded or changed. That is its finding, not proof. Downstream. Reported effects included the public portal of a national product-labelling system (its core system kept working), a car-sharing service, a payments service and several regional government sites. The car-sharing service restored service the same afternoon by moving to another contractor. Contract. The provider declared force majeure and paused billing for services not yet restored. Commentators noted that contractual compensation generally follows fees paid, not business losses.

Seven lessons: 1. Keep one recovery copy that production's administrators cannot delete. If the credentials that run production can also delete the backups, the catalogue and the keys, the backup sits inside the blast radius. Keep one copy under separate credentials, isolated or immutable, and restore from it on a schedule. 2. Protect the map, not only the data. Catalogues, configuration, inventories and decryption keys need their own copy and their own protection. 3. A provider's percentage is not your recovery. Measure recovery per workload against the time you set. ISO 22301:2019 asks the organisation to analyse the impact of disruption on each activity, including its dependencies on suppliers (8.2.2), and to choose continuity strategies (8.3). 4. Know your exit before you need it. That works only when a second route is prepared: who controls the domain, how DNS changes, where a copy of the content sits and who can approve the move. 5. Read the contract against your loss before the outage. If a day offline costs more than a year of fees, a fee-based remedy and your continuity plan do not match. 6. Test a complete rebuild, not a file restore. A rebuild covers servers, configuration, DNS, certificates, secrets and data, and ends with a business user confirming it works. Time it once. 7. Plan your own communications and keep a loss register. Your customers should hear from you sooner, over channels that do not depend on the provider. Keep a register of what was lost and when, for compensation and insurance.

The article also gives a tabletop scenario for next week: a fictional provider, four injects. T+0: sites and mail are down and the provider's status page does not load. T+3 hours: the provider confirms unauthorised access to its management systems and says data was erased, with no restore time. T+24 hours: the provider says backups exist for only some customers and yours is not confirmed. T+72 hours: the second environment is running from your copy and a customer asks for evidence that nothing was altered and when service will be complete. Record the gap between your recovery time target and the real rebuild time as a finding, with an owner and a date.
