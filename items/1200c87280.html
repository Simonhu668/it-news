供应商提前期、工厂日历、工艺路线、供应商条款与物料属性中的小错误，能在 ERP 项目宣布上线很久之后仍悄悄扭曲计划。主数据之所以危险，是因为系统看起来一切正常：交易错误很明显，因为有人完不成任务或单据不平；而主数据错误往往藏而不露，因为系统仍在运转。采购建议生成了，产能计划生成了，成本结果算出来了，交期也出现了，输出看起来权威，因为系统处理得正确。问题在上游：供应商提前期仍写 21 天，实际已变成 35 天；工厂日历漏掉一个当地假期；工艺路线假设某工序按现产品系列从未达到的标准运行；辅料最小起订量已过时；物料单位换算错了。这些错误不会让 ERP 崩溃，只会悄悄扭曲建立在其上的决策。并非每个主数据字段都值得同等治理投入。应优先治理改变以下结果的数据：承诺日期、物料就绪、产能需求、采购紧迫性、成本或利润、质量要求、包装与发运就绪。高杠杆主数据通常包括款式与物料属性、供应商参数、提前期、颜色尺码结构、工序顺序、工作中心产能、效率假设、工厂日历、包装规则与物流参数，具体清单取决于业务模式，但原则一致：治理传播范围广的假设。提前期是最具影响力的计划输入之一，也最容易被放任过时：供应商多年前报的标准提前期，实际表现可能已随物料系列、颜色、季节、订货量或生产地点而变化；更好的做法是拿主数据提前期与近期实际里程碑对比，目标不是每次延迟都改主数据，而是识别持续偏差以支持受控更新；负责人还须清楚提前期的起止事件定义，“下单到到货”与“供应商确认到工厂收货”是不同的口径。错误的工厂日历会扭曲其上建立的全部计划：漏掉假期或错误的班次日历会改变可用产能、计划完工与交期计算；多工厂集团更复杂，日历按国家、工厂、部门甚至生产区域而异，本地团队需要维护真实日历的权限，集团则需要看到日历是否最新；日历治理是运营工作，不应依赖一年一次的 IT 清理。工艺路线的详细程度应与业务决策需要匹配：工序太笼统，产能与成本无法反映真实约束；每个微步骤都建模，维护成本高昂，用户也会因路线从不最新而不再信任；正确的详细程度是能可靠支撑计划、执行与成本决策的程度；产品结构变化、引入新设备、工作外包或实际表现反复偏离标准时应复核路线。物料属性在工厂解读不一致时会产生风险：宽度、颜色、成分、整理、缩水、检验类别、计量单位与包装都可能重要；相似物料描述不一会导致重复建档，关键属性缺失会让用户在自由文本中重新输入背景信息。每个高杠杆数据域都应有负责人、定义、单一来源、更新触发条件、复核频率与异常处理路径；例如采购可拥有供应商提前期，计划可拥有工厂日历与工作中心，工艺部门可拥有工艺路线标准，商品或产品团队可拥有款式属性，质量可拥有检验分类。负责人不必亲自编辑每条记录，但要对规则负责。衡量质量时应避免只看完整率：字段可以 100% 填满但仍然是错的。更好的指标包括：因提前期错误导致的计划异常数量、重复手工改期、重复物料建档、因路线错误导致的产能返工、因最小起订量或包装假设错误导致的采购修改、因日历错误导致的订单重排。这些指标把数据治理与运营价值连接起来。最好的纠正发生在证明数据错误的业务事件附近：集中治理适用于标准，本地运营往往最先看到漂移；工厂应能标记不现实的工艺路线标准，采购应能标记已变化的供应商提前期，仓库应能标记计量单位混淆；治理模型需要一条受控路径来快速复核与纠正这些问题，否则用户会学会绕开主数据而不是改善主数据。每月复核一次影响最大的计划、采购、生产与成本异常，问哪些是由错误假设而非一次性事件造成的，然后选少量主数据纠正措施并指定负责人，跟踪纠正后同一异常是否重现，由此在实际运营与系统假设之间建立反馈回路。更新应与业务事件挂钩：新面料来源触发提前期、幅宽、可用收率与检验假设的复核；新工厂触发日历、产能、效率与工艺检查；买家包装规则变化触发相关订单与包装属性更新。审批模型应与风险匹配：低影响描述字段可留在运营用户手中，驱动计划、成本、采购或合规的字段需要更强的归属；跟踪各主数据字段的手工覆盖频率也很有用——如果计划员反复覆盖提前期、效率假设或物料参数，系统其实在说主数据已不可信，重复覆盖应生成复核任务，而不是成为被接受的背景噪声。最后，生效日期应明确：新标准不应悄悄改写按旧假设计划的订单逻辑；在业务流程要求时，保留订单创建或下达时适用的是哪个版本，方差分析才可信。文章还指出：用户变通做法是主数据不再值得信任的证据，应要求用户展示三起主数据导致错误决策的近期案例，分类是过期数值、定义不清、缺失本地参数还是从不更新字段的流程问题，然后修复维护机制，而不只是修复三条记录；可信的主数据会赢得采用，因为用户看到纠正措施持续存在并改善了计划，而反复被覆盖的主数据只会让用户觉得电子表格更安全。

原文

Small master-data errors in lead times, calendars, routings, supplier terms and material attributes can quietly distort garment plans long after the ERP project is declared live. Master-data errors are dangerous because the system can still look perfectly normal. Transactional errors are obvious because somebody cannot complete a task or a document does not balance. Master-data errors often remain hidden because the system still works. A purchase suggestion is created. A capacity plan is generated. A costing result is calculated. A delivery date appears. The output looks authoritative because the system processed it correctly. The problem is upstream. A supplier lead time still says 21 days when the actual pattern has become 35. A factory calendar ignores a local holiday. A routing assumes an operation runs at a standard that the current product family never achieves. A trim minimum order quantity is outdated. A material unit conversion is wrong. Those errors do not crash the ERP. They quietly distort the decisions built on top of it. Some master data has far more operational leverage than the rest. Not every master-data field deserves the same governance effort. Prioritize data that changes one or more of these outcomes: promised date, material readiness, capacity requirement, procurement urgency, cost or margin, quality requirement, packing and shipping readiness. For a garment business, high-leverage master data typically includes style and material attributes, supplier parameters, lead times, color-size structures, operation sequences, work-center capacities, efficiency assumptions, factory calendars, packaging rules and logistics parameters. The exact list depends on the business model, but the principle is consistent: govern the assumptions that propagate widely. Lead time is one of the most influential planning inputs and one of the easiest to leave stale. A supplier may have quoted a standard lead time years ago. Actual performance may now vary by material family, color, season, order quantity or production location. A better approach compares master lead time with recent actual milestones. The objective is not to change the master after every late delivery. It is to identify persistent deviation that justifies a controlled update. The owner should know what event starts and ends the lead time. PO to arrival is different from supplier confirmation to factory receipt. A wrong factory calendar distorts every plan built on top of it. A missing holiday or incorrect shift calendar can alter available capacity, planned completion and delivery calculations. In multi-factory groups this becomes more complex because calendars differ by country, site, department and sometimes production area. Local teams need authority to maintain real calendars, while the group needs visibility that the calendars are current. Calendar governance is operational work. It should not depend on an annual IT cleanup. Routing detail should match the decisions the business needs to make. If operations are too generic, capacity and costing may not reflect real constraints. If every micro-step is modeled, maintenance becomes expensive and users may stop trusting the route because it is never current. The correct level of detail is the level required to support planning, execution and cost decisions reliably. Review routes when product construction changes, new equipment is introduced, work is subcontracted or actual performance repeatedly diverges from the standard. Material attributes become risky when factories interpret them differently. Width, color, composition, finish, shrinkage, inspection class, unit of measure and packaging can all matter. If similar materials are described differently, users create duplicates. If critical attributes are omitted, teams re-enter context in free text. Master data needs an owner because reality keeps changing. Every high-leverage data domain should have an owner, a definition, a source of truth, an update trigger, a review frequency, and an exception path. For example, sourcing may own supplier lead times. Planning may own factory calendars and work centers. Industrial engineering may own routing standards. Merchandising or product teams may own style attributes. Quality may own inspection classifications. The owner is not expected to edit every record personally. The owner is accountable for the rules. Operational exceptions reveal weak master data better than abstract completeness scores. Avoid measuring quality only by completion percentage. A field can be 100 percent populated and still be wrong. Better indicators include the number of planning exceptions caused by wrong lead time, repeated manual date overrides, duplicate material creation, capacity rework caused by route errors, purchase amendments caused by wrong MOQ or pack assumptions, and orders replanned because calendars were wrong. Those measures connect data governance to operating value. The best correction happens close to the business event that proves the data wrong. Central governance is useful for standards. Local operations are often the first to see drift. A factory should be able to flag that a routing standard is unrealistic. Purchasing should be able to flag that a supplier lead time has changed. Warehouse should be able to flag unit-of-measure confusion. The governance model needs a controlled path to review and correct those issues quickly. Otherwise users learn to work around the master rather than improve it. Once a month, review the highest-impact exceptions from planning, purchasing, production and costing. Ask which ones were caused by bad assumptions rather than one-off events. Then choose a small number of master-data corrections with clear owners. Track whether the same exception recurs after the change. Change points are where master data needs the most discipline. A new fabric source should trigger a review of lead time, width, usable yield and inspection assumptions. A new factory should trigger calendar, capacity, efficiency and routing checks. A changed buyer packing rule should trigger the relevant order and packaging attributes. The approval model should match the risk. Low-impact descriptive fields can remain close to the operational user. Fields that drive planning, costing, procurement or compliance need stronger ownership. It is also useful to track manual override frequency by master-data field. If planners repeatedly override a lead time, efficiency assumption or material parameter, the system is telling you that the master is no longer credible. Repeated overrides should create a review task rather than becoming accepted background noise. Finally, make effective dates explicit. A new standard should not silently rewrite the logic behind orders that were planned under the old assumption. Where the business process requires it, preserve which version applied when the order was created or released. User workarounds are evidence that the master no longer deserves trust. Ask users to show three recent cases where the master produced a bad decision. Classify whether the issue came from an outdated value, unclear definition, missing local parameter or a process that never updates the field. Then fix the maintenance mechanism, not only the three records. A trustworthy master earns adoption because users see that corrections persist and improve planning.
